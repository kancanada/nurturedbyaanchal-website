Our mothers fought for stability. We fought for identity.

My most watched Reel ever is about why modern women feel like they're failing. You're not. The rules didn't get replaced, they piled up.

By Aanchal Bathija · November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

I've posted a lot of Reels. None of them has travelled the way this one did. As I write this it has close to 800,000 views and more than 120 comments.

So I want to slow down and say properly what I was reacting to, and why I think it hit so many of us.

Watch the Reel on Instagram

The line that started it

The Reel is me reacting to a short podcast clip about what "having it all" meant for our mothers' generation compared to ours. The line I put on screen sums up where I landed: our mothers fought for stability, we fought for identity, and now we're drowning in both.

Our mothers, and their mothers, were often fighting for something very basic. A stable home. Peace behind closed doors. Enough money to get the kids through school. A lot of them didn't get to choose their education or their work, and keeping the family steady was the whole job.

Our generation fought for something different. A voice. An identity outside the home. A career that's ours. A seat at every table. And we got a lot of it, which matters.

The rules didn't get replaced. They just piled up.

Where it went wrong

Here's what I said in my caption, and what I think so many women recognised: somewhere along the way, the new rules didn't replace the old ones. They just got stacked on top.

So now the expectation is that you have a thriving career, and you keep fit, and you practise self-care, and you manage the household, and you work on your mental health, all at the same time. And the final rule, the one nobody says out loud: make it look effortless.

None of those things is bad on its own. I work full time in home insurance. I go to the gym when I can steal the time. I'm raising a two-year-old with my husband. I chose these things. But when every one of them comes with its own standard of "doing it right", the total weight is more than any one person was built to carry.

You're not failing

This is the part I most wanted women to hear: you aren't failing, and you aren't falling behind. The blueprint just got impossibly heavy.

When you're carrying something that heavy, it's very easy to decide the problem is you. That if you were more organised, more disciplined, more grateful, you'd be able to do it all. I don't think that's true. I think the list itself is the problem.

Our mothers had one set of expectations. We were handed theirs and a whole new set, with the same 24 hours in a day. That's not a personal failure. That's maths.

So what do we do with that?

I don't think the answer is to give up on what our generation fought for. I like having a career. I like having a life that's mine. I don't want my son to grow up thinking women have to choose between a family and an identity.

But I do think we get to put some things down. A few questions that help me look at the list honestly:

• Whose rule is this? Is this something I actually value, or something I absorbed from social media, family or work culture?
• Does it need to be done well, or just done? Not everything deserves my best. Some things deserve "good enough".
• Who else can carry this? A partner, a parent, a friend, a paid service, a simpler version of the task.
• What would I tell my best friend? We are so much kinder to other women than we are to ourselves.

When I run my own list through those questions, some things stay and some things shrink. Not everything on the list is a rule I chose. A lot of it is a rule I inherited, or one I picked up from scrolling at 11pm.

A note on "effortless"

The hidden rule in all of this is that it should look easy. The perfect lunchbox, the glowing skin, the calm voice at bedtime, the promotion, the workout selfie. We show the result and hide the scramble, and then every other woman compares her messy behind-the-scenes to our highlight reel.

I share my life online, so I know I'm part of that too. That's one reason I like talking about the scramble out loud. Nothing about raising a toddler while working full time is effortless, and I think more of us should say so. It doesn't make you less capable. It makes you honest.

Rewriting the blueprint together

I also don't want this to become one more thing women have to fix alone. The heavy blueprint is held up by a lot of people: workplaces that expect you to work like you have no kids and parent like you have no job, families who still expect the home to run on one person's effort, and a culture that celebrates the woman who does everything instead of asking why she has to.

Partners can take whole jobs off the list, not just "help". Workplaces can stop treating flexibility like a favour. And the rest of us can stop clapping for burnout dressed up as productivity.

Our mothers fought so we could have choices. Maybe our fight is making sure those choices don't come with an impossible price.
