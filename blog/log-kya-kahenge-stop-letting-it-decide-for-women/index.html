"Log kya kahenge": the most expensive excuse we have

Why I think "what will people say" holds women back more than almost anything else, and what real support for a woman's ambition looks like.

By Aanchal Bathija · November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Four words. Every South Asian girl has heard them before she's old enough to understand them: log kya kahenge. What will people say?

I reacted to a scene from a Zee5 show on my Reels where a family is wrestling with exactly this question, and the caption I wrote was simple: log kya kahenge is the most expensive excuse we use to hold women back. It's one of my most watched Reels this month, so I want to say the rest of what I think here, with more room than a short video gives me.

Why I call it expensive

We usually talk about log kya kahenge like it's a small thing. A bit of gossip. A raised eyebrow at a family function. Something you roll your eyes at and move on from.

But think about what it actually costs. The degree a girl didn't choose because it wasn't "suitable". The job offer in another city, or another country, that she turned down because a daughter-in-law shouldn't be that far away. The marriage she stayed in longer than she should have. The business idea that stayed an idea. The workout she skipped, the outfit she changed, the opinion she swallowed at the dinner table.

None of those things show up on a bill. But they add up to a whole life that was a little smaller than it needed to be. That's the price, and it's almost always the woman who pays it.

The neighbours won't pay your bills, build your career or live with your regrets.

Society will have an opinion either way

Here's the part that took me a long time to see. Society always has an opinion. Not sometimes. Always.

If you take the big opportunity abroad, people will talk about how you left your parents. If you stay inside the lines they drew for you, people will still talk, about why you aren't married yet, why you're married but not pregnant yet, why you went back to work, why you didn't. There is no version of your life that the aunties will fully approve of.

So if they're going to talk anyway, the only real question is: whose life are you living while they do it?

I share my life online as a working mom. I work full time in home insurance, I'm raising a two-year-old, and I go to the gym when I can steal the time. Every one of those choices is something someone, somewhere, has an opinion about. I've learned that I can listen to an opinion without handing it the steering wheel.

Who "log" actually are

It's worth asking, honestly: who are these log? When you write the list down, it's usually a handful of relatives, a few neighbours and some people from the community WhatsApp group. People who will not be there when the bills are due, when the career stalls, or when you're lying awake at 2am wondering what would have happened if you'd said yes.

Often they're not even bad people. A lot of the time it's fear talking. Our parents' generation grew up where reputation was protection, and anything unfamiliar felt risky. I understand that. But understanding where a fear comes from doesn't mean you have to let it decide your future.

Women carry it the most

Boys hear log kya kahenge too, but let's be honest about who carries more of it. A son who moves abroad for work is ambitious. A daughter who does the same is selfish. A man who goes back to work after a baby is a provider. A woman who does it is asked how she can leave her child all day.

And the pressure doesn't stop at marriage. It just changes address. Now it's the in-laws' neighbours, the in-laws' relatives, the new community. Every life stage comes with a new set of people whose opinion you're supposed to manage.

That's why I framed my Reel the way I did. This isn't only about one family scene in a show. It's about how many women's futures quietly get decided by people who have no stake in how those futures turn out.

What real support looks like

In my caption I wrote that real support means backing a woman's ambition even when the world questions it. I want to be specific about what that means, because "support" is easy to say.

• For parents: when your daughter tells you about an opportunity, let your first question be "how can we help?" and not "what will people think?" You can share your worries. Just don't make them her ceiling.
• For partners: stand next to her in the room when the comments come. Don't leave her to defend her choices alone at family gatherings.
• For in-laws: remember what it felt like when it was you being judged. You get to decide that it stops with you.
• For friends: cheer loudly. A woman who is going against the grain needs at least a few people who are openly on her side.

And for us, the women: notice when log kya kahenge is the real reason behind a "no". Sometimes there's a good, practical reason not to do something. But sometimes, if you're honest, the only reason is what people will say. That's the one I want us to stop accepting.

Start with one decision

You don't have to blow up your whole life to start. Pick one decision, big or small, that you've been holding back only because of other people's opinions. The class you want to take. The job you want to apply for. The trip. The way you want to raise your child. The boundary you want to set.

Make that one decision for yourself, and see what actually happens. Usually, people talk for a while, and then they find someone else to talk about. You, on the other hand, get to live with the result. Make sure it's a result you chose.

I want my son to grow up in a home where the question is "is this right for us?" and not "what will people say?" That's a habit that starts with the grown-ups.
