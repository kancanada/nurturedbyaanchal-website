Your peace is expensive. Stop giving out free passes.

If emotional damage had an hourly rate, women would be rich. Why I think moms especially need to stop handing out free access to their peace.

By Aanchal Bathija · November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

One of my Reels had a single line on screen: if emotional damage had an hourly rate, women would be rich.

I meant it as a joke, but only half. In the caption I wrote: your peace is expensive. Stop giving free passes to people who constantly drain you. I want to talk about that second half properly.

The invisible invoice

Think about the hours that never show up on any timesheet. The phone call that leaves you tense for the rest of the evening. The family group chat that turns every decision you make into a debate. The colleague who only comes to you with problems. The relative who always has a comment about your weight, your parenting, your job or your marriage.

Each of those costs something. Energy. Patience. Sleep. The mood you bring home to your kids. If you added it all up and sent an invoice, a lot of women would be owed a fortune.

Your peace is not free. Stop acting like it is.

Why women hand out free passes

Most of us were raised to keep the peace, not to protect our own. Be nice. Adjust. Don't make a scene. Don't upset the elders. Smile through it and talk about it later, or better yet, never.

So we absorb things. We keep answering the calls that ruin our day. We keep explaining ourselves to people who've already decided they don't agree. We keep showing up for people who don't show up for us. And then we wonder why we're so tired.

For moms, there's an extra layer. When you're responsible for small people, your energy isn't only yours. It's also the patience you have at bath time, the calm you bring to bedtime, the attention you give to a toddler who wants to show you the same block tower for the fifth time. When someone drains you, your family often pays for it too.

What a boundary actually is

"Set boundaries" has become one of those phrases everyone says and nobody explains. Here's how I think about it. A boundary isn't a wall, and it isn't a punishment. It's simply a decision about what you'll allow to take from you, and what you'll do when someone crosses that line.

It can be very small:

• Not answering a call during dinner and calling back when you're ready.
• Leaving a group chat on mute.
• Saying "I've already decided, but thank you" and then changing the subject.
• Spending less time with someone who always leaves you feeling worse.
• Not explaining a decision that was yours to make in the first place.

You don't need a dramatic confrontation. Most boundaries are quiet. You just stop paying the invoice.

Signs your peace is being overdrawn

Sometimes we don't notice how much we're paying until the account is empty. A few signs worth paying attention to:

• You feel your shoulders tighten when a certain name lights up your phone.
• You rehearse conversations in your head long after they're over.
• You're short with your kids or partner after spending time with a particular person.
• You say yes, then spend the next week resenting it.
• You feel like you need to recover after a visit that was supposed to be fun.

None of these mean that person is a villain. They just mean the relationship, as it is right now, costs you more than it gives. That's useful information. You can decide what to do with it.

Start with one small boundary

If this feels big, pick one thing. One call you'll let go to voicemail. One topic you'll stop discussing with one person. One evening a week where your phone goes on Do Not Disturb after the kids are in bed. Small boundaries are easier to keep, and every one you keep makes the next one easier.

Expect some pushback. People who are used to unlimited access usually notice when it changes. That doesn't mean you're doing it wrong. It usually means it's working.

But what if it's family?

This is the hard part, especially in South Asian families, where so much is built on obligation and respect for elders. Protecting your peace can feel like disrespect, and the guilt can be loud.

I don't think protecting your peace means cutting everyone off. It can mean loving someone and still deciding how much access they get to you. You can respect your elders and still not hand them a vote on every part of your life. You can pick up the phone and still end the call when it turns.

And if you're married, this is something to talk about together. Partners can protect each other's peace, especially when the draining comments are coming from their side of the family.

Your peace is part of your family's peace

It's easy to feel that protecting your energy is selfish. I think it's one of the most practical things a mom can do. A calmer you shows up better for everything else that matters: your work, your relationships, and the small, ordinary moments with your kids.

So this is your permission slip. You don't owe everyone unlimited access to you. Your peace is expensive. Charge accordingly.
