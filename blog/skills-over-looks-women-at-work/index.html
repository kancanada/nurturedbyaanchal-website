Stop judging women at work by how they look

Hair colour, clothes, a mom bun: none of it tells you if a woman is good at her job. Why I want us to stop confusing appearance with capability.

By Aanchal Bathija · November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

A couple of weeks ago I posted a Reel with one simple line on it: stop judging women by their appearance at work. It became one of my most watched Reels this month, which tells me this is not a small thing for a lot of you.

So I want to say it properly here, with more room than a 30-second video gives me.

Watch the Reel on Instagram

Appearance is not a job skill

Corporate culture still has a habit that nobody likes to admit. Before anyone looks at a woman's track record, they've already formed an opinion about her from how she looks. Her hair colour. Her outfit. Whether she wears makeup or doesn't. Whether she looks "put together" or looks like she did a daycare drop-off at 7:45 and came straight in.

None of that tells you whether she can lead a project, handle a difficult client or solve the problem nobody else wanted to touch. Expertise, leadership and drive don't have a dress code. And yet, so often, the first impression gets decided long before her work has a chance to speak.

Looks are a first impression. Skills are the whole story.

It shows up in quiet ways

This kind of bias is rarely loud. Nobody says "we didn't give her the project because of her hair". It's quieter than that. It's who gets spoken over in a meeting. Who gets asked to take notes. Who gets described as "too much" or "not serious enough" in a conversation she's not part of. Whose idea only sounds good when someone else repeats it.

And for women, the rules move. Dress up, and you're trying too hard. Dress simply, and you don't care. Wear something from your own culture, and suddenly it's a talking point. There's no outfit that wins that game, so I've stopped trying to play it.

Moms get a version of this too

I work full time in home insurance and I'm raising a two-year-old. Some mornings, every working mom looks exactly like what she is: a woman who has already done a full shift at home before she logs on.

Motherhood adds its own layer. There's an assumption that if you look tired, you're less committed. That if you leave on time for pickup, you care less. That the mom who comes back from leave is somehow less sharp than before she left.

I don't believe any of that. If anything, the moms I know have become ruthless about getting things done, because the clock at the end of the day is not optional. You learn to prioritise fast when there's a little person waiting for you.

Let your work be loud

In my Reel I said: let your performance speak so loudly that their assumptions become completely irrelevant. I still believe that, and I want to be careful about what I mean by it.

I don't mean that it's your job to fix other people's bias by working twice as hard. That's not fair, and it's exactly how so many women burn out. What I mean is: don't shrink yourself to fit their idea of what a professional looks like. Show up as you are, do excellent work, and keep a record of it.

A few things that can help:

• Write down your wins. Not for anyone else, for you. When review time comes, you'll have the facts ready instead of trying to remember.
• Say your idea out loud, and if someone repeats it later, calmly say "yes, that's what I suggested earlier".
• Find one person at work who will say your name in rooms you're not in. A good mentor or manager matters more than any outfit.
• Stop apologising for things that don't need an apology, like a daycare call or leaving on time.

Why it matters more than it seems

A snap judgement in a hallway doesn't stay in the hallway. It turns into who gets invited to the client meeting, who gets the stretch project, who gets considered for the promotion, and whose raise gets a little smaller. Over a few years, those small calls add up to a very different career.

And when women see that pattern, many of them change themselves to avoid it. They spend money and time they don't have on looking a certain way, or they tone down the parts of themselves that don't fit the mould. That's energy that could have gone into the actual work, or into their families, or into themselves.

What I want my son to see

My son is growing up watching his mom go to work every day. I want him to grow up thinking it's completely normal to judge people by what they do and how they treat others, not by what they look like walking in the door.

That lesson doesn't come from a speech. It comes from what he hears at the dinner table, how we talk about the people we work with, and what we admire out loud. Kids notice everything.

This is for the people doing the judging too

Most of us have done this at some point, if we're honest. Made a snap judgement about a colleague, a new hire, another mom at daycare, based on nothing more than how she looked that day.

So here's a small challenge for anyone who manages people, hires people, or just sits in meetings: the next time you form an opinion about a woman before she's said a word, notice it. Then wait for her work. Judge that instead.

And to the women reading this: your hair colour, your clothes, your skin, your accent, your mom bun, none of it is a measure of what you're capable of. Let them be surprised. That's their problem, not yours.
