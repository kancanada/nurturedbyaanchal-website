StatCan: B.C. child care staff least likely to stay put

A new Statistics Canada study tracks how many early childhood educators stay in child care. Pay, pensions and unions made the biggest difference.

By Aanchal Bathija · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read

Statistics Canada released a new study on November 25 that looks at a question every parent waiting on a daycare spot has wondered about: how many early childhood educators actually stay in child care, and what makes them leave?

The study, by Youjin Choi and Kristyn Frank, was published in StatCan's Economic and Social Reports. It followed about 23,000 early childhood educators and assistants who worked in the child care services industry in 2021 and checked where they were working one year later. The researchers linked tax records with the 2021 census to do it.

The headline numbers

Across Canada, 83% of early childhood educators and assistants were still working in child care a year later, and 75% were still with the same employer, StatCan's Daily summary reported. That employer figure is about the same as the average for all paid workers in Canada.

Of those who left child care, the most common next stop was elementary and secondary schools, at 22%. The study notes this could reflect more opportunities, or better wages and benefits, in the school system.

B.C. had the lowest rate of staying with the same employer

This is the part that stood out for families here. In British Columbia, 68% of early childhood educators and assistants were still with the same employer a year later, the lowest of the 10 provinces. Saskatchewan and Alberta were close behind at 69%, Ontario was at 75%, and Quebec was highest at 79%.

On staying in the child care field at all, B.C. was in the middle of the pack at 80%. Quebec was highest at 88% and Saskatchewan lowest at 76%. The authors suggest Quebec's results may be linked to higher wages and benefits for educators there and to its province-wide child care system, which has been in place since 1997.

Pay and benefits made the biggest difference

The pattern in the data is very clear. Among educators earning less than $20,000 a year at their main job, 64% stayed in child care and only 51% stayed with their employer. Among those earning $40,000 to $59,999, 94% stayed in the field and 91% stayed with their employer. Those figures are in 2024 dollars.

Benefits mattered too. Of educators who paid union dues, 90% stayed in the industry, compared with 82% of those who didn't. Of those who made pension contributions, 90% stayed, compared with 79% of those who didn't. Experience also helped: educators with more than 10 years in their job had a 94% industry retention rate, while those with one to two years were at 74%.

The researchers say these findings can help shape policies to keep educators in the sector, which supports the goals of the Canada-wide $10-a-day child care system.

What the study can't tell us

There's an important catch. The data only follows workers from 2021 to 2022, before most of the federal-provincial $10-a-day agreements were fully in place, so it doesn't show whether retention has improved since. It also can't measure things like stress or job satisfaction. StatCan says a new Canadian Child Care Worker Survey should help fill those gaps.

For context on what B.C. families are paying, the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives' annual fee survey from August found B.C. infant fees in the cities it tracked ranged from $28 a day in Kelowna to $52 in Richmond, the highest of the 35 cities in the report. Its authors wrote that "most child care fees in Canada are not at $10 a day."

What I think

For a working mom, child care isn't only about getting a spot. It's about whether the educator your child trusts will still be there in six months. Your workday depends on those people. This study puts numbers on something parents feel. If we want more child care spaces in B.C., we also need to pay and treat the people who staff them well enough that they stay.
